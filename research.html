Research

My research develops and applies econometric methods for measuring productivity, technical change, and causal effects when firms choose inputs and technologies in response to productivity they observe and the econometrician does not. The productivity papers use flexible production models, first-order conditions, and control functions to separate objects that are usually collapsed into a single residual. The methodological papers ask what those separations cost in identifying assumptions, and what happens when the assumptions fail.

Awarded the Best Third-Year Paper Award, Department of Economics, Binghamton University (2025).

Job Market Paper

Job Market Paper

Digitalization, Factor-Biased Productivity, and Market Power

with Subal C. Kumbhakar

Request Paper Research Statement

Abstract

This paper studies how digitalization is related to factor-biased productivity and market power. We distinguish labor-augmenting productivity from Hicks-neutral productivity and extend a production-function approach to allow for imperfect competition in the output market. The framework uses the ratio of labor and materials first-order conditions to remove the markup term common to both, isolating labor-augmenting productivity, while separate transition equations characterize Hicks-neutral productivity and markup dynamics. We apply the model to Spanish manufacturing firms over 2000–2019 using a multidimensional index of digitalization. In the perfect-competition benchmark, lagged digitalization is positively associated with both labor-augmenting and Hicks-neutral productivity in the pooled sample, with a stronger labor-augmenting response among small firms. Allowing for market power leaves the labor-augmenting results broadly positive but produces markup effects that vary substantially across firms. The results show why digitalization, factor bias, and market power should be studied jointly rather than through a single productivity residual.

Data and Method

Encuesta Sobre Estrategias Empresariales, 2000–2019; 2,256 firms and 17,610 firm-year observations after cleaning. Digitalization is measured with an index built from 13 survey items covering IT technologies, digital human capital, automation, and stakeholder-facing technologies, rather than a single ICT-spending proxy. Real output and materials use firm-specific Paasche price indices. Inference uses a firm-block bootstrap that repeats every estimation stage.

Publications

Causal Forests versus Penalized Splines for Heterogeneous Treatment Effects

with Ivan Korolev and Nency Dhameja

Economics Letters, 268 (2026), 113205.

Journal Version Working Paper (PDF) ScienceDirect Replication Code

Abstract

This paper compares causal forests with varying-coefficient penalized spline estimators for heterogeneous treatment effects. We conduct simulations with randomized treatment assignment that vary heterogeneity, predictor dimension, and discrete nuisance structure. Performance is measured by out-of-sample mean squared error. Forests perform best in Wager–Athey designs with sharp treatment-effect transitions. Penalized splines tend to dominate when heterogeneity is smooth, when the baseline mean and treatment effect differ in structure, and in mixed designs with continuous, binary, and group-level covariates. As group cardinality rises, forest performance deteriorates, while mgcv random-effect smooths handle group structure effectively and remain easy to implement.

Working Papers

Mediation Analysis in Production Functions with Unobserved Productivity

with Subal C. Kumbhakar

Under review, Economics Letters

Request Paper

Abstract

We decompose treatment effects in production settings into direct and input-mediated channels. Standard mediation methods fail because firms choose inputs in response to productivity observed by the firm but unobserved by the econometrician, biasing conventional decompositions. We develop a structural framework that consistently identifies direct, indirect, and total treatment effects while accounting for endogenous mediator choice. Monte Carlo experiments show that our feasible ACF-style estimator accurately recovers these effects, whereas omitting productivity from the mediator equation substantially biases the indirect effect. We apply the method to 17,277 observations from 2,762 Spanish manufacturing firms, using labor as the mediator and lagged digitalization as the treatment.

The Scale-Technology Paradox: Technological Progress and Productivity Decline in Nigerian Banking

with Subal C. Kumbhakar

Revise and resubmit, Economic Modelling

Request Paper

Abstract

Nigeria's banking sector expanded and adopted new technologies after consolidation, yet whether these changes improved bank productivity remains unclear. Existing banking studies emphasize static efficiency or advanced-economy evidence, leaving multi-output productivity dynamics in developing economies underexplored. This study provides bank-level evidence for Nigeria that separates productivity change from technical change, using annual reports for 17 commercial banks from 2000 to 2023 and a multi-output input distance function with a control function approach. Productivity fell by 3.5 percent per year on average (median 3.1 percent), with larger and internationally licensed banks recording steeper losses. Technical change was cost-reducing, but returns to scale averaged 1.46, and loans were the dominant cost driver. The findings reveal a scale-technology paradox: modernization shifted the technological frontier outward, but underused scale, costly lending, and asset-quality pressures prevented those gains from becoming higher productivity.

Work in Progress

Regularization in Translog Production Models

Solo-authored

Flexible translog specifications buy generality with severe multicollinearity among interaction and curvature terms. This project evaluates Lasso, Ridge, Elastic Net, principal component regression, and partial least squares by what they do to the economic objects, namely elasticities, substitution, and returns to scale, rather than by predictive fit.

Artificial Intelligence and Spatial Factor-Biased Productivity

AI may change the relative productivity of labor and capital inside an adopting firm, but knowledge flows, worker mobility, and input-output linkages carry the shock past the firm's boundary. This project estimates direct and spatially transmitted components of factor augmentation across industries and local labor markets.
