Teaching

I teach economics as a progression from intuition to formal reasoning to credible evidence. Students should leave a course able to name the economic mechanism in a problem, represent it with an appropriate model, and say what evidence would separate that model's predictions from a competing explanation.

In empirical courses this means the estimand and the source of identifying variation come before the estimator. I build that understanding through the economic question, the assumptions an estimator needs, and the formal argument that connects them, and I use simulation to make the consequences concrete: students generate data with a known parameter, recover it under a correct design, then watch omitted variables, measurement error, or selection move the estimate away from the truth. Bias stops being a formula and becomes a research problem they can recognize. The same instinct carries into reproducible R workflows, where the deliverable is a script another researcher could run start to finish.

Mentoring and Student Support

Office hours are where I find out where a student's reasoning actually breaks: the economic intuition, the algebra, the statistical assumption, or the code. I use questions and small counterexamples to let students repair the chain themselves, then ask them to explain the result back in their own words. This matters most in introductory econometrics, where confidence collapses quickly when the theory and the software appear disconnected.

My prior work in data science also shapes what I ask of students: careful data construction, reproducible workflows, and an explicit distinction between predictive performance and causal evidence.
